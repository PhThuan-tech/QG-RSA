\begin{abstract}
Quantum machine-learning components are often reported to improve classical
pipelines by margins smaller than the run-to-run variation of those
pipelines. We study whether quantum components help exemplar-free
class-incremental learning, starting from a strong pre-trained baseline,
under a protocol designed to resolve effects of a few tenths of a point.
The protocol has four parts: paired seeds; every inference head evaluated
on the same network, with digit-level identity checks; decision rules
written before each run; and, for every quantum component, a classical
counterpart with the same inputs. We test five placements: a projected
quantum kernel as a steering metric, as a separation metric and as a
classifier feature map; and a pretty-good measurement over class density
matrices, used alone and fused with the classifier. None outperforms its
classical counterpart. The kernel loses up to 17 points through
concentration of its local reduced states. The measurement matches ridge
regression on the same statistics ($-0.04$ over 12 runs), as theory
predicts. We prove that circuits placed after the encoding and the usual
state regularisation cannot change the measurement. The study also yields
a positive result that is not quantum: fusing the classifier with a
calibrated score computed from the stored class statistics raises final
accuracy on four benchmarks, by $+0.38$ to $+0.67$ points.
\keywords{Quantum machine learning \and Class-incremental learning \and
Quantum state discrimination \and Reproducibility}
\end{abstract}
